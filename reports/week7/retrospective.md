## What went well

1. **Features were implemented exactly as intended, with no misunderstandings.** The shared understanding of mechanics held up throughout the Sprint - what was planned matched what was built.
2. **Task status was consistently updated in the feature list.** All team members marked their progress on time, which made it easy to track everyone's status at a glance without needing separate check-ins.
3. **Communication was fast and responsive.** When someone had a question about implementation, other team members typically answered almost immediately, which saved time that would otherwise have been lost waiting for clarification.

## What did not go well

1. **The team overestimated its own capacity and took on too many tasks.** The Sprint Backlog ended up overloaded relative to what the team could realistically deliver.
2. **Not all planned work was finished in time for the customer meeting.** As a result of the overloaded backlog, some PBIs were not ready by the Sprint Review.
3. **Programmers started implementing "could have" items before "must have" ones.** Prioritization was not followed during implementation, meaning effort went into lower-priority features while higher-priority ones were still incomplete when the deadline arrived.

## What changed compared to Sprint 4

Assets were integrated into the game immediately after being created. As soon as the artist finished a piece of art, it was added to the game right away, preventing the previous Sprint's problem of finished assets being forgotten.

## Action points

1. **Base Sprint scope on realistic capacity, not optimistic estimates.** Use velocity from previous Sprints as a reference when deciding how much to commit to, rather than assuming best-case throughput.
2. **Enforce MoSCoW prioritization during implementation, not just planning.** Programmers should implement "must have" PBIs first and only move to "could have" items once all higher-priority work is complete, so a tight deadline doesn't put core scope at risk.
